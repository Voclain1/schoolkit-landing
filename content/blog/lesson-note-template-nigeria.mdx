Every Nigerian teacher knows the Sunday-night routine: the week's lesson notes are due on the head teacher's desk on Monday morning, signed and ready.

This guide gives you a **lesson note template in the format Nigerian schools use**, explains what goes in each section, and walks through a **complete written example** you can model your own on.

## The Lesson Note Format

Most Nigerian primary and secondary schools use a format close to this one. Check your school's version — some add or rename a heading — but the sections below cover what head teachers and inspectors look for.

| Section | What to write |
|---|---|
| Subject | e.g. Mathematics |
| Class | e.g. Primary 4 |
| Term / Week | e.g. First term, Week 6 |
| Date | The day the lesson is taught |
| Duration | e.g. 40 minutes |
| Topic | The scheme-of-work topic |
| Sub-topic | The part of the topic taught in this lesson |
| Number in class | Average or actual number of pupils |
| Average age | e.g. 9 years |
| Behavioural objectives | What pupils should be able to do by the end |
| Instructional materials | What you will use to teach |
| Reference materials | Textbooks and sources, with pages |
| Previous knowledge (entry behaviour) | What pupils already know that this lesson builds on |
| Set induction (introduction) | How you will start the lesson and get attention |
| Presentation | The lesson itself, in numbered steps: teacher's activity and pupils' activity |
| Evaluation | Questions that check each objective |
| Summary / conclusion | The key points, in a few lines |
| Assignment | Homework that practises the lesson |

## Writing Each Section Well

### Behavioural objectives

This is the section head teachers check first. Each objective should:

- start with **"By the end of the lesson, pupils should be able to…"**
- use an **action verb you can see or check**: identify, list, state, calculate, draw, label, explain, compare, classify, demonstrate,
- be **specific to this lesson**, not the whole topic.

Avoid *know*, *understand*, *appreciate* and *learn* — you can't observe them.

| Weak objective | Strong objective |
|---|---|
| Pupils should understand fractions | Pupils should be able to write two equivalent fractions for a given fraction |
| Pupils should know the parts of a plant | Pupils should be able to label the root, stem, leaf and flower on a diagram |
| Pupils should learn about rivers in Nigeria | Pupils should be able to name three major rivers in Nigeria and the states they flow through |

### Instructional materials

Name real, specific items: "a chart showing a fraction wall", "12 oranges cut into halves and quarters", "a map of Nigeria". "Charts and textbook" on its own is the most common reason a note is sent back.

### Previous knowledge (entry behaviour)

One or two sentences on what pupils already know: "Pupils can identify halves and quarters of a whole shape." It shows the lesson follows from the last one.

### Set induction

A short, concrete start: a question, a story, an object or a quick game that leads into the topic. Two to five minutes is enough.

### Presentation

Write it as **numbered steps**, each with the **teacher's activity** and the **pupils' activity**. This is the heart of the note — it should be detailed enough that another teacher could take the class from it.

### Evaluation

Write one question for each objective. If an objective has no question, either the objective or the evaluation is wrong.

### Assignment

Practice of what was taught, not new content. Give page and exercise numbers if it comes from the textbook.

## Complete Example: Primary 4 Mathematics

| | |
|---|---|
| **Subject** | Mathematics |
| **Class** | Primary 4 |
| **Term / Week** | First term, Week 6 |
| **Duration** | 40 minutes |
| **Topic** | Fractions |
| **Sub-topic** | Equivalent fractions |
| **Number in class** | 30 |
| **Average age** | 9 years |

**Behavioural objectives:** By the end of the lesson, pupils should be able to:

1. explain that equivalent fractions name the same part of a whole;
2. write two equivalent fractions for a given fraction by multiplying the numerator and denominator by the same number;
3. identify which of two fractions are equivalent using a fraction wall.

**Instructional materials:** a fraction wall chart (whole, halves, quarters, eighths); paper strips for each group; four oranges cut into halves and quarters.

**Reference materials:** the class's approved Primary 4 Mathematics textbook (chapter on fractions).

**Previous knowledge:** Pupils can identify and write halves, quarters and eighths of a whole shape.

**Set induction:** The teacher cuts one orange into 2 halves and another into 4 quarters, then asks: "If Ada eats one half and Bayo eats two quarters, who ate more?" Pupils discuss and notice they ate the same amount.

**Presentation:**

1. **Step 1 — Same amount, different names.** *Teacher:* places one half and two quarters of an orange side by side and writes ½ = 2/4 on the board. *Pupils:* compare the pieces and say why they are the same amount.
2. **Step 2 — Using the fraction wall.** *Teacher:* shows on the fraction wall that ½ is the same length as 2/4 and 4/8, and explains these are called equivalent fractions. *Pupils:* fold their paper strips into halves, quarters and eighths and shade ½, 2/4 and 4/8 to check.
3. **Step 3 — The multiplying rule.** *Teacher:* shows that 1×2 / 2×2 = 2/4 and 1×4 / 2×4 = 4/8, and states the rule: multiply the top and bottom by the same number. *Pupils:* copy the rule and work through ⅓ → 2/6 → 3/9 with the teacher.
4. **Step 4 — Practice.** *Teacher:* writes ¼, ⅔ and ⅗ on the board and moves round the groups. *Pupils:* write two equivalent fractions for each in their exercise books.

**Evaluation:**

1. What are equivalent fractions?
2. Write two fractions equivalent to ¾.
3. Use the fraction wall: is 2/4 equivalent to 3/8? Explain.

**Summary:** Equivalent fractions name the same part of a whole. To find one, multiply the numerator and the denominator by the same number.

**Assignment:** Write three fractions equivalent to each of ½, ⅓ and ⅖.

## Tips That Get Notes Approved First Time

- **Follow the scheme of work.** The topic and week should match your school's scheme of work for the term.
- **One period, one sub-topic.** A note that tries to cover a whole topic in 40 minutes is the second most common reason for a rewrite.
- **Match evaluation to objectives**, question for question.
- **Write the content, not just the method.** If the step says "teacher explains photosynthesis", write the explanation.
- **Prepare a week ahead.** Notes written the night before rarely have real instructional materials.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between a lesson note and a lesson plan?

In Nigerian schools the terms are often used interchangeably. Strictly, a lesson plan is the outline of how a lesson will run, while a lesson note also contains the content the teacher will teach. Most schools ask for one document that does both, in the format shown in this guide.

### What are behavioural objectives in a lesson note?

They state what pupils should be able to do by the end of the lesson, using action verbs that can be observed and checked — such as identify, list, calculate, explain or draw. "Understand" and "know" are avoided because they cannot be measured.

### How many objectives should a lesson note have?

Two to four is usual for a single period. Each should be something you will actually check in the evaluation.

### How long should a lesson note be?

Long enough that another teacher could teach the lesson from it. For a 35–40 minute period that is usually one to two pages.

### Who approves lesson notes?

In most schools the head teacher, vice principal (academics) or head of department reviews and signs lesson notes each week before the lessons are taught.
